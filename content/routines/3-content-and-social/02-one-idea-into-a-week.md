# One Idea Into A Week

Takes the one good thing you made and turns it into every other format, so you make it once.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Runs** | Mondays, 10:00 |
| **Cron** | `0 10 * * 1` |
| **Connectors** | Google Drive, Gmail |
| **Takes** | One run a week |

---

## Making things twice is the real cost

You record a twenty-minute video. It is good. Then it sits there, because turning it into a post means watching it back, and turning it into an email means writing the email, and you have other work.

So the video gets one life instead of six, and next week you make another thing from scratch.

This routine reads whatever you made — a video transcript, a long post, a call recording, an article — and writes the other five formats from it. Same idea, genuinely rewritten for each place, not the same paragraph pasted six times with different hashtags.

## Set it up

1. **New routine**, name it `One Idea Into A Week`
2. In Google Drive, make a folder called `Source content`
3. Paste the instructions and **put the folder name at the bottom**
4. **Schedule** → **Weekly** → Monday, 10:00
5. **Connectors**: Google Drive and Gmail
6. **Create**

Then the only thing you do each week is drop one file in the folder.

## Instructions

```
Turn the newest source file into a week of content.

Look in the Google Drive folder named at the bottom. Take the most
recently added or edited document. If it is empty, or nothing has been
added since your last run, send nothing at all and stop.

Read it and identify:
- the single strongest idea in it, in one sentence
- the two or three supporting points that idea rests on
- any specific number, example, story or quote in the source
- the part a reader would argue with

Then write six pieces from it.

1. SHORT POST, under 80 words. Lead with the part someone would argue
   with. No preamble.
2. LONG POST, 250 to 400 words, structured as: the problem, why the
   obvious fix does not work, what to do instead, what changes as a
   result.
3. EMAIL, 200 to 300 words, written to one person. Subject line
   included. It may reference the source directly and link to it.
4. VIDEO SCRIPT, 60 to 90 seconds spoken. Give the first spoken line,
   three beats, and the last line. Mark where something should be shown
   on screen rather than said.
5. CAROUSEL or slide outline, 6 to 8 slides, under 15 words a slide,
   with the first slide being the hook and the last being the point.
6. THREE STANDALONE LINES that could each be a post on their own. These
   should be the sharpest sentences in the whole set.

Rules:

- Every piece must use the real specifics from the source. If the source
  names a number, the pieces name that number. Do not invent examples,
  statistics, customers or outcomes that are not in the source.
- The six pieces must not share an opening line. If two of them open the
  same way, rewrite one.
- Do not add a call to action unless the source has one.
- If the source is thin — under roughly 300 words of substance — say so,
  produce the short post and the three lines only, and skip the rest.
  Padding a thin idea into six formats is how this stops being useful.

Email everything to the connected account, subject "This week from: [name
of the source file]".

--- EDIT BELOW THIS LINE ---

Everything in [square brackets] below is an example. Replace it with
your own and delete the brackets. If anything is still in brackets when
this runs, it is not a real setting — ignore it and say so at the top of
your output rather than treating the example as an instruction.

Google Drive folder to watch: Source content
Voice notes — how I write, and what I would never say: [e.g. plain and
  direct, short sentences, no exclamation marks, never "game-changer"]
```

## What to put in the folder

Anything with an idea in it:

- a video transcript (YouTube gives you one free — open the video, **...** → **Show transcript**)
- a long post you already wrote
- notes from a customer call
- a support answer you found yourself writing for the third time
- a voice memo you had transcribed
- an article you wrote and never published

The better the source, the better all six pieces. A rambling transcript with one real insight in it still works — finding the insight is the part it is good at.

## Before your first run

**Put something in the folder first.** An empty folder produces an empty run, which looks like the routine is broken.

**It will refuse to pad a thin source, on purpose.** That instruction is there because the alternative is six confident pieces about nothing, and posting those costs you more than posting nothing.

**These are first drafts.** Every one of them should be read and cut before it goes out. The routine saves you the blank page, not the judgement.

## What a good run looks like

> **From: onboarding-call-oct.txt**
>
> The strongest idea: people do not abandon setup because it is hard, they abandon it because they cannot tell whether it worked.
>
> **Short post** — *"Nobody quits because the setup was difficult. They quit because they finished and still weren't sure it had worked."*
>
> **Email** — Subject: The step everybody skips
> *You know that moment when you have followed every instruction and you still don't know if you did it right? That is where people leave...*
>
> **Video script** — First line: *"There is one screen missing from almost every setup flow I look at."*
> Beat 1 — show the flow, no narration.
> Beat 2 — the moment of doubt.
> Beat 3 — what a confirmation screen changes.
> Last line: *"It is one screen. It is always worth it."*
>
> **Three standalone lines**
> — Finished is not the same as confident.
> — Every abandoned setup has a screen missing right before the exit.
> — Your instructions are fine. Your confirmation is missing.

## Prefer Slack instead of email?

If your drafts live in Slack, send them there.

In the instructions, replace the "Email everything" sentence with:

> Post the result as a single message to the #content channel in Slack.

Change `#content` to your channel, then swap the **Gmail** connector for
**Slack**. Keep Google Drive — that is where the source comes from.

## When it goes wrong

**It sends the same source twice.** It has no memory of what it already processed. The fix is a second folder: make one called `Source content — done` and add a line to the instructions telling it to move the file there after processing. Or simply clear the folder yourself each week.

**The six pieces all sound identical.** Usually a thin source. Check whether the document actually contains an argument or just a description.

**It invents a customer story that never happened.** The "do not invent" instruction is there to stop this. If it still drifts, harden it: "If you need an example that is not in the source, write [EXAMPLE NEEDED] instead of inventing one."

**Nothing arrives at all.** That is the correct behaviour when nothing new was added. Check the folder before assuming a fault.
