# Interview Pack Builder

The questions to ask each candidate, drawn from their own application, ready the morning of.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Runs** | Daily, 07:00 |
| **Cron** | `0 7 * * *` |
| **Connectors** | Google Calendar, Gmail, Google Drive |
| **Takes** | One run a day |

---

## Most interviews are a waste of everyone's hour

Not because the interviewer is bad at it, but because they walk in with the same eight general questions they always use, having skimmed the CV in the corridor. So the hour gets spent establishing things the application already said, and the candidate who is best at interviews wins.

A good interview does the opposite: it spends the whole hour on the two or three things the application genuinely did not answer. That requires having read the application properly and worked out what those things are, which is a forty-minute job per candidate that nobody does the day they have four interviews.

This routine does it the morning of. Same structure for every candidate, questions specific to each.

## Set it up

1. **New routine**, name it `Interview Pack Builder`
2. Paste the instructions and **fill in the bottom section**
3. **Schedule** → **Daily** → 07:00
4. **Connectors**: Google Calendar, Gmail, Google Drive
5. **Create**

## Instructions

```
Build the interview packs for today.

Read today's calendar for anything matching the interview titles listed
at the bottom. If there are none, send nothing and stop.

For each interview, find the candidate's application in email and read
the job description document named below. Then build the pack.

1. THE BASICS. Time, length, who is interviewing, which stage this is.
   If there was an earlier interview and there are notes on it in email
   or in Drive, summarise what was covered so it is not covered again.

2. WHAT THEIR APPLICATION SAYS, against each requirement in order. One
   line each: what they evidenced, and whether it was direct or
   indirect.

3. THE THREE THINGS THIS INTERVIEW IS FOR. The most important gaps
   between what the job needs and what the application establishes.
   Three, not eight. An hour spent properly on three things beats an
   hour spent lightly on eight, and the eight-question interview is what
   everybody does by default.

4. THE QUESTIONS. For each of the three, give:
   - the opening question, specific to this candidate and referring to
     something they actually wrote
   - two follow-ups that get past a rehearsed answer
   - what a real answer would contain, so it is possible to tell the
     difference between somebody who has done the thing and somebody
     who has read about it

   Ask about what they did, not what they would do. Hypotheticals test
   articulacy; "tell me about the last time" tests experience.

5. WHAT THEY WILL PROBABLY ASK, based on the role, the stage, and
   anything in their application that suggests what they care about. And
   an honest answer for each — including the awkward ones about pay,
   hours, security, and what happened to the last person in the role.
   Being unprepared for those is how good candidates are lost.

6. WHAT TO TELL THEM. The two or three things about this job that are
   genuinely worth knowing and are not in the advert, including the hard
   parts. A candidate who takes the job on an accurate picture stays.

7. ONE THING TO CHECK. Anything factual in the application worth
   confirming: dates, scope, whether they did something or were on a
   team that did.

Keep every pack to the same shape, so candidates are comparable
afterwards.

Do not comment on or infer anything about age, sex, gender, race,
nationality, ethnicity, religion, disability, health, family or marital
status, sexual orientation, or where somebody is from or was educated.
Do not suggest any question about these. If an application volunteers
any of it, do not repeat it.

Do not score or rank anybody, and do not suggest an outcome.

Email everything to the connected account, subject "Interviews today —
[date]", with a one-line summary of each at the top.

--- EDIT BELOW THIS LINE ---

Calendar events that are interviews — the titles or keywords:
Job description document:
Requirements, in order:
Things about this job that are genuinely hard, and should be said out
loud:
What we pay, and what is negotiable:
```

## Before your first run

**Fill in the hard parts of the job honestly.** Section 6 is the one that reduces early leavers. A candidate who finds out in month two that the role includes something nobody mentioned will leave, and you will have spent the hiring cost twice.

**Three things, not eight.** This is the instruction that changes interviews most. Most interviewers cover a lot of ground shallowly because they have not decided what the hour is for.

**Answer the pay question before the interview.** Section 5 forces you to know what you would say. The worst version of that conversation is the improvised one.

**The same shape every time is doing real work.** Comparing candidates afterwards is only possible if the packs are structured identically. Varying the format is how the most recent interview ends up feeling like the best one.

## What a good run looks like

> **Today: 2 interviews.**
> **11:00** A. Okonkwo — second stage, finance role.
> **15:00** J. Meyer — first stage, finance role.
>
> ---
>
> **11:00 — A. Okonkwo (60 min), second stage**
> *First interview covered:* the payroll experience and the migration. Notes say the system choice was not discussed.
>
> *Against the requirements:* payroll for 10+ — direct. Statutory reporting — direct. Managing an external accountant — **not addressed at any stage**. On-site days — direct.
>
> **This interview is for three things:**
>
> **1. The accountant relationship — the biggest gap.**
> Opening: *"Your application covers the internal side thoroughly, but not the accountant. Tell me about the last time you had to push back on something your external accountant told you."*
> Follow-ups: *"What did they say?"* / *"What happened at the next year end?"*
> A real answer names a specific disagreement and a specific outcome. A rehearsed one talks about the importance of communication.
>
> **2. Whether they chose the payroll system or inherited it.** ...
>
> **3. The five-month gap between roles.** Ask neutrally, once, and move on.
>
> **They will probably ask:** whether this role is replacing somebody (it is — say why plainly), whether the two on-site days are fixed (they are), and about the salary range (£X to £Y, the top of it is for someone who can take the accountant relationship off you from day one).
>
> **Tell them:** month end is genuinely heavy for about four days. The systems are older than they would expect. Nobody else in the building does this job, so it is solitary.

## When it goes wrong

**It builds packs for your internal meetings.** Be specific in the calendar keywords.

**It cannot find the application.** The candidate applied under a different address, or through a platform that does not forward the CV. Say in the bottom section where applications actually live.

**The questions are generic.** That means the application was thin or not found. Good questions come from their own words, so when there are no words, it falls back to textbook questions.

**It suggests questions about personal circumstances.** Strengthen the exclusion list with whatever appeared. This one matters legally as well as ethically.

**Every pack has the same three things.** Possibly correct if the applications are similar. If not, check that the requirements list is specific enough to produce different gaps for different people.
