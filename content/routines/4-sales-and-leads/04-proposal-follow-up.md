# Proposal Follow-Up

Every quote you sent that has gone quiet, and the follow-up that does not grovel.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Runs** | Daily, 09:00 |
| **Cron** | `0 9 * * *` |
| **Connectors** | Gmail. Google Drive optional |
| **Takes** | One run a day |

---

## Most quotes are lost to silence, not to no

Ask anybody who sells services what happened to the proposals they lost, and the honest answer is usually "I don't know, they stopped replying". Not a rejection. Just nothing.

Some of those were never going to buy. But a meaningful share went quiet because the person who asked for the quote had four other things on, meant to come back to it, and did not. They are not avoiding you. They have forgotten, and a short email on the right day brings them straight back.

The reason this does not happen is that following up requires remembering who you sent what to and when — exactly the kind of tracking that falls apart the moment you are busy, which is also when you sent the most quotes.

## Set it up

1. **New routine**, name it `Proposal Follow-Up`
2. Paste the instructions and **set your follow-up schedule at the bottom**
3. **Schedule** → **Daily** → 09:00
4. **Connectors**: Gmail. Add Google Drive if your proposals live there as documents
5. **Create**

## Instructions

```
Find the proposals and quotes that have gone quiet, and draft the
follow-ups that are due today.

Search sent email from the last 90 days for messages that contain a
quote, a proposal, a price, an estimate or a scope of work. The phrases
at the bottom will help you identify ours.

For each, check whether the recipient has replied since.

Ignore any thread where they replied with a clear decision, either way.
A no is a finished conversation and chasing it costs you the
relationship.

For each thread with no reply, work out how many days it has been and
which follow-up is due today according to the schedule at the bottom.
Only draft the one that is due today. Do not draft ahead.

For each due follow-up, give me:
- who, and their company
- what was quoted, and for how much
- when it was sent, and how many days of silence
- which follow-up number this is
- whether anything in the original thread suggests why it stalled —
  a mention of a budget cycle, a holiday, another decision-maker, an
  unanswered question of theirs

Then draft the email. Under 80 words. Rules for these, in order of
importance:

- Never apologise for following up. It signals that you think you are an
  imposition, and it invites the reader to agree.
- Never say "just checking in", "circling back", "bumping this" or
  "touching base". These say nothing and everybody recognises them as
  filler.
- Give them something or ask them something. A follow-up that contains
  neither is a request for emotional labour.
- Follow-up 1 assumes they are busy. Follow-up 2 offers something
  genuinely useful, or asks the one question that would unblock a
  decision. Follow-up 3 closes the loop and gives them an easy exit —
  and it must genuinely be the last one.

Then list separately anything past the final follow-up with no reply.
Those are closed. Say so, so I stop thinking about them.

Never send anything. Draft only.

If nothing is due today, say so in one line.
```

## Why 4, 11 and 25

Four days is long enough that they have had a proper look and short enough that you are still in their head. Eleven catches the person who meant to reply the following week. Twenty-five is the one nobody sends, and it is the one that most often gets a "sorry — yes, let's do it", because by then whatever was blocking them has usually resolved itself.

Change the numbers to suit your sales cycle. Longer cycles want 7, 21, 45. What matters is that there are three, spaced unevenly, and that the third one really is the last.

## Before your first run

**It drafts and never sends.** Every one of these goes to somebody who is deciding whether to give you money. They are worth ten seconds of your attention each.

**The first run will surface old quotes.** Read the list before drafting anything — some of them you will be glad to be reminded of, and some you will want to add to a permanent ignore list.

**Follow-up 3 must actually be the last.** If you keep going past it, you become the thing people feel guilty about rather than the thing they buy. The routine closes them out for you precisely so that you can let go.

## What a good run looks like

> **2 follow-ups due today. 1 closed out.**
>
> **Follow-up 1 — Priya Shah, Northfield Makes.** £2,400 fit-out quote, sent 4 days ago. Her last message mentioned a board meeting "next week".
> > Subject: Northfield — the fit-out quote
> > Priya — sending this back up in case it got buried before your board meeting. Nothing needed from you today. If it helps to have the numbers split by phase for that conversation, say the word and I will send it over this afternoon.
>
> **Follow-up 2 — Tom Reilly, Reilly & Co.** £800 audit, sent 11 days ago. He asked about timing in the original thread and never got a direct answer.
> > Subject: The bit I didn't answer
> > Tom — you asked how long this takes and I gave you a vague answer. Properly: two weeks from the go-ahead, and I have space from the 6th. If the timing is the thing holding it up, that is worth knowing either way.
>
> **Closed out**: Alex M., £1,100, sent 31 days ago, three follow-ups, no reply. Stop thinking about this one.

## Reading proposals from Google Drive

If you send proposals as Drive documents rather than in the body of an email, connect Google Drive and add one line: *"Also check the Proposals folder in Drive for documents created in the last 90 days, and match them to the email they were sent with."*

That gets you the real figures rather than whatever was summarised in the covering email.

## When it goes wrong

**It thinks every email with a number in it is a proposal.** Fill in the phrases section with something distinctive that genuinely appears in your quotes — a heading you always use, a standard line about terms.

**It drafts follow-ups to people who said no.** Their no was probably soft — "not right now", "we'll come back to this". Add: "Treat 'not now', 'maybe later' and 'we've decided to hold off' as a decision. Do not follow up."

**The drafts sound needy anyway.** The banned-phrase list is doing most of the work here. If it still creeps in, add your own least favourite phrases to it.

**It drafts three follow-ups at once for an old thread.** The "only draft the one due today" instruction is the guard. On the first run it may still catch up on old threads — that is a one-off.
