# Monthly Opportunity Scan

Once a month, the three things you could do that you are not currently considering.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Runs** | 1st of the month, 08:00 |
| **Cron** | `0 8 1 * *` |
| **Connectors** | Gmail, Stripe. Google Drive optional |
| **Takes** | One run a month |

---

## The options you cannot see from inside

Running a business narrows your field of view, and it does it gradually enough that you never notice. You get good at the thing you do, your customers are the customers you already have, and the set of things you consider doing next is drawn entirely from what you are already doing.

Meanwhile, the evidence for what you should do next is sitting in your own data. The thing eleven people asked for and nobody wrote down. The customer segment that quietly pays twice as much and churns half as often. The product people buy and then never mention again.

This routine looks at your own business once a month as though it had just arrived, with no attachment to the current plan, and reports what it finds.

4. **Connectors**: Gmail and Stripe. Add Google Drive if you keep notes or a plan
5. **Create**

## Instructions

```
Look at this business from the outside and report what is being missed.

Read Stripe for the last 6 months, and email for the last 3. Then report
five things.

1. THE DEMAND NOBODY WROTE DOWN. Every time a customer or prospect asked
   for something we do not sell. Group them by what was actually wanted
   and count each group. Quote two or three real requests per group.
   Then rank the groups by how many people asked and by what those
   people pay. Something eleven customers have asked for is a product
   decision, not a support ticket, and the only reason it is not treated
   as one is that nobody ever counted.

2. THE CUSTOMERS WHO ARE DIFFERENT. Look for any group in the customer
   base that behaves unlike the rest: pays more, stays longer, needs
   less support, buys faster, refers more. Group by anything visible —
   industry, size, where they came from, which product they bought
   first, which country. Say how they are different and how many of them
   there are. If we have thirty of these and they are twice as valuable,
   that is the most useful sentence in this report.

3. WHAT IS QUIETLY WORKING. Any product, plan, price point or channel
   that performs better than its share of attention. Something that is
   8% of effort and 30% of revenue is being under-invested in, and it is
   almost always something nobody has thought about for a year.

4. WHAT IS QUIETLY NOT. The reverse. Anything taking real effort and
   returning little. Be specific about the effort — the number of
   support threads it generates, the number of customers on it.

5. THE THING THAT KEEPS NOT HAPPENING. Anything mentioned repeatedly in
   our own notes and email as something we intend to do, that has not
   been done. Say how long it has been mentioned. Do not moralise about
   it — sometimes the right conclusion is that it should be dropped
   rather than done.

Then, and only then, THREE OPPORTUNITIES. For each:
- what it is, in one sentence
- the evidence for it from above, with the actual numbers
- what the smallest possible test of it would be — something doable in a
  fortnight, not a project
- what would have to be true for it to work
- what it would cost us to be wrong

Be conservative. An opportunity supported by three data points is a
hunch and should be labelled one. Say clearly which of the three is best
supported and which is the most speculative.

Do not suggest anything on the "already decided against" list at the
bottom. Do not suggest generic business advice — every suggestion must
be traceable to something specific in this month's data.

Email the result to the connected account, subject "Opportunity scan —
[month]".

--- EDIT BELOW THIS LINE ---

Everything in [square brackets] below is an example. Replace it with
your own and delete the brackets. If anything is still in brackets when
this runs, it is not a real setting — ignore it and say so at the top of
your output rather than treating the example as an instruction.

What we sell now: [e.g. stock control software for small workshops,
  £49/mo]
What we have already decided against, and why: [e.g. a free tier — tried
  it in 2025, it filled support with people who never bought]
What we are already working on: [e.g. scheduled exports, the help centre]
Constraints — time, money, skills, anything that rules options out: [e.g.
  two people, no developer, under £500 a month to try anything]
```

## Before your first run

**Fill in "already decided against" properly.** Otherwise it will suggest the thing you spent six months failing at in 2024, and you will stop trusting the report. Include the why — it helps it understand the shape of what you are avoiding.

**Section 2 is usually the most valuable.** Almost every small business has a hidden segment that is worth more than the average, and almost none of them know which one, because they have never grouped their customers by anything other than revenue.

**Read it once, act on nothing for a week.** This report is designed to be interesting, which makes it dangerous in the first hour. The small-test requirement exists to stop a monthly report from turning into a monthly change of direction.

**Three months in a row saying the same thing is the signal.** One month is a hypothesis.

## What a good run looks like

> **1. Demand nobody wrote down**
> **Scheduled exports — 11 requests in 3 months.** Those 11 customers pay £1,640/mo between them, 19% of revenue.
> *"is there any way to have this just land in my inbox every monday"*
> *"I end up doing the export by hand every month which feels silly"*
> Second group: a phone view, 6 requests. Third: multi-currency, 3 requests, all from one country.
>
> **2. The customers who are different**
> 23 customers came from one specific directory listing. They pay the same, but their median tenure is 14 months against 7 for everybody else, and they open 40% fewer support threads. They are 11% of customers and, on lifetime value, about 21% of the business.
>
> **3. Quietly working**
> The annual plan is 14% of customers and 38% of revenue, and it is not mentioned anywhere on the pricing page above the fold.
>
> **4. Quietly not**
> The lowest tier: 31% of customers, 9% of revenue, and 44% of all support threads.
>
> **5. Keeps not happening**
> A help centre has been mentioned in your own notes in five of the last six months.
>
> **Three opportunities**
>
> **A — Scheduled exports. Best supported.** 11 requests from customers worth 19% of revenue, and a competitor shipped it last month. *Smallest test:* offer it manually to those 11 this week and see how many say yes. *Wrong if:* they asked casually and will not change behaviour. *Cost of being wrong:* a fortnight.
>
> **B — Do more of whatever produced the directory customers.** 23 customers, twice the tenure, half the support. *Smallest test:* find out how that listing happened and whether there are five more like it. *Wrong if:* the listing is not repeatable.
>
> **C — Rethink the lowest tier. Most speculative.** It consumes 44% of support for 9% of revenue. *Wrong if:* it is the top of your funnel and people upgrade from it — check that before touching it. *Cost of being wrong:* high, and slow to discover.

## When it goes wrong

**It suggests generic strategy.** The traceability rule is the guard. Harden it: "Every opportunity must cite a number or a quote from sections 1 to 5. No citation, no suggestion."

**It suggests what you are already doing.** Fill in "what we are already working on".

**Every month suggests the same three things.** That is probably the report being right and you not acting. It is also a sign to add anything you have consciously chosen not to do to the decided-against list, with the reason.

**Section 2 finds nothing.** You may genuinely have a uniform customer base, or Stripe may not be carrying enough information to group by. Adding a note of where each customer came from, even roughly, makes this section work.
